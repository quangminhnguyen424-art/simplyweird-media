# Research: why does ice float?

## Demand check
- TED-Ed "Why does ice float in water?" (George Zaidan, 3:56): long-running evergreen lesson, opens with solid argon sinking in liquid argon.
- SciShow "Why does ice float?" (Hank Green, 2014): about 10 min.
- Many school and kids explainers rank for this question, so it is a steady search topic.
Our angle: a 90-second version with a household hook (wax instead of argon), the dance-floor vs. hexagon picture, and the "4 °C" lake twist. We do not reuse their scripts, titles or visuals.

## Facts used (with sources)
- Most solids sink in their own liquid; ice is an exception. Sources: TED-Ed transcript (https://amara.org/en/videos/5HYCsVsuqS2p/en/585341/), UT Marine Science Institute (https://utmsi.utexas.edu/science-and-the-sea/radio-program/floating-ice/)
- A water molecule is one oxygen and two hydrogens; molecules link by hydrogen bonds. Source: Live Science (https://www.livescience.com/chemistry/why-does-ice-float)
- In liquid water, hydrogen bonds constantly break and re-form. Source: Lumen Learning (https://courses.lumenlearning.com/umes-cheminter/?p=2714)
- In ice, the bonds lock into a hexagonal framework with open space in the middle of the hexagons. Sources: Lumen Learning (https://courses.lumenlearning.com/umes-cheminter/chapter/the-structure-of-ice/), Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/summary/ice)
- Ice density is about 0.917 g/cm³ against about 1.00 for liquid water, so ice is about 9% less dense. Sources: Ice Ih (https://en.wikipedia-on-ipfs.org/wiki/Ice_Ih), Children's Museum of Indianapolis (https://www.childrensmuseum.org/stories/why-does-ice-float)
- Water is densest at about 4 °C. Source: LibreTexts CK-12 (https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_Chemistry/Introductory_Chemistry_(CK-12)/15:_Water/15.02:_Structure_of_Ice)
- Lakes freeze at the top and stay liquid underneath, so aquatic life survives. Sources: WorldAtlas (https://www.worldatlas.com/articles/why-does-ice-float.html), CORDIS EU research (https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/454697-why-does-ice-float)
- Snowflakes are six-sided because of ice's hexagonal crystal structure. Sources: Live Science, Britannica (above)
